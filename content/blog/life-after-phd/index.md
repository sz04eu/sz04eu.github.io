Alan Watts once said that the meaning of life is just to be alive. It is so plain and so obvious, yet everybody rushes around in a panic as if it were necessary to achieve something far beyond themselves.

I left the office in October. For a few days, I really didn't know where to go and what to do. When a long journey ends, the apartment gets quiet, and the calendar becomes strangely empty. I made a cup of coffee, sat by the window, and thought: Okay, now what?

I want to continue doing work that interests me, but I am learning to approach it with a completely different mindset. Modern society often teaches us that suffering, stress, and high productivity equal virtue, while rest and play are shameful. But life was never meant to be a constant struggle for a score.

I've been watching videos about Stoicism. A few core principles really stood out to me:

**Dichotomy of Control:** Focus only on what you can control. The philosopher Epictetus used the image of an archer. You practice, steady your posture, and release the arrow. After that, the wind decides. The archer's job isn't to control where the arrow lands, but simply to make a good shot.

**Desire:** Do not crave things completely outside your control, or you hand your peace of mind over to others.

**Action and Reflection:** Reflect daily on your actions to see if you lived well, taking time each evening to look inward.

**Judgment:** Pause before your first reaction takes over. Ask yourself: Is this impression true? Do I really need to react this way?

Instead of feeling exhausted and anxious, I am choosing a player mindset: relaxing, letting go of inner friction, and living fully in the present.

I saw this clearly through my AuDHD son. For two years, he has gone to the pool every single day after school. At first, he couldn't even pass Swim 1. But he kept showing up, day after day, simply enjoying the water without worrying about perfection. Now, he is preparing for his Bronze Medallion. Watching him taught me what it means to hand yourself another chance, not because you have to prove anything to the world, but because you genuinely love the process.

The past weeks after my PhD are the first time in a long while that I don't feel like I'm answering to a scoreboard. Instead, I see life as an avatar experience. In a game, when your character faces a challenge or a setback, the person sitting behind the screen remains safe and whole. The avatar experiences the quest, but the player holds the peace.

You didn't come into this world to hand in a perfect final exam. You came here for a unique, one-of-a-kind experience. You don't need to grip the wheel with white knuckles all the time. The goal is simply to keep playing and enjoy the game.
