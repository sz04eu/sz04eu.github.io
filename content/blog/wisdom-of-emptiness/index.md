## 1. The Paradox of Atrophy
An older gentleman my wife cares for has lived with a brain tumor for over two decades. Years ago, surgeons removed most of it, but intentionally left a small piece because it was dangerously close to his cranial nerves.

Recently, a follow-up scan revealed something astonishing: due to age-related brain atrophy, his shrinking brain created extra physical space. This unexpected "emptiness" naturally relieved the pressure of the remaining tumor on his nerves.

I couldn't stop thinking about this. The physical "loss" of brain tissue actually created a life-saving margin. The emptiness saved him.

This story struck me deeply. At 43 years old, as I step away from my Ph.D. in Computer Science to begin a new chapter, I am experiencing my own version of space. But instead of a triumphant celebration, I was immediately greeted by a massive wave: the AI revolution.

It feels like the invention of the steam engine. The world is accelerating at a dizzying pace. Charles Dickens' famous opening line from A Tale of Two Cities, "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times," feels very real right now. 

When my daily routine changed, my life suddenly slammed to a halt. I found myself with empty space. And initially, I was terrified of it.

## 2. The Collision of Emptiness and Guilt
Why was I terrified? Because our society often teaches us a toxic equation: Busyness equals results, and results equal worth.

For years, my body was used to high-pressure output and tight deadlines. When I suddenly stopped, I experienced severe withdrawal symptoms. I felt panic, anxiety, and a profound sense of self-doubt.

When life is completely filled with work, we often lose the energy to confront our deeper emotions. We use busyness as a shield, terrified that if we stop, we will realize we aren't constantly achieving. 

I was trying to use busyness to prove my existence, forgetting that life itself deserves to be supported unconditionally. I was so afraid of the void that I wanted to fill it immediately. But then, I remembered the older man and the space in his brain. Maybe my emptiness wasn't a threat. Maybe it was a necessary margin.

## 3. Eastern Philosophy and Spiritual Anchoring
If the shrinking of the older man’s brain created physical space to relieve pressure, perhaps my sudden emptiness was doing the same for my soul. To understand this, I turned to older wisdom.

I thought of Chapter 11 of the Tao Te Ching by Laozi: "Shape clay into a vessel; it is the space within that makes it useful." A cup is useful precisely because it is empty. If it were solid clay, it could hold nothing. The same applies to our lives. Without blank space, such as quiet afternoons and unstructured days, inspiration, awareness, and relaxation have nowhere to take root. The art of *liubai* (leaving blank space) in Chinese painting is a perfect metaphor: the meaning is found in the untouched paper, not just the ink.

I also found myself reflecting on this while hiking in nature. Watching the wildlife and the birds flying freely overhead, I remembered a verse from the Gospel of Matthew (6:26): "Look at the birds of the air; they do not sow or reap or store away in barns..."

Birds do not have our complex, exhausting self-worth systems. They never question their right to exist, nor do they feel guilty for resting on a branch. They simply are. Humans, on the other hand, try to use busyness to prove our existence, forgetting that life itself is already enough.

It was time to redefine value. Rest is not the absence of productivity; it is a vital part of a well-lived life. Simply enjoying the present moment is, in itself, a valuable output.

## 4. Returning to the Physical Body and Future Anchors
Once I stopped fighting the emptiness, my daily routine began to shift. The world outside is using AI to accelerate everything. To counter this digital void and anxiety, I decided to lean into my primitive physical senses.

My new core task is simple: I spend a few hours every day walking, swimming, cooking, and reading. To a driven person, this might look like wasting time. But I now realize these are not idle distractions. They are how I build strength for the future. Engaging my physical body by feeling the water when I swim and smelling the food when I cook is my way of grounding myself in reality.

Of course, I haven't checked out completely. I still spend a little time every day exploring AI. But the energy has shifted. I am no longer studying them out of survival anxiety or the fear of being left behind. I am treating them as low-pressure interests and quiet skill upgrades.

Charles Dickens wrote that it was the best of times and the worst of times. In this fascinating era, it is easy to feel overwhelmed by the speed of change. But maybe the best thing we can do is allow ourselves to stop, take a breath, and look forward.

## 5. Conclusion
We are all carrying our own challenges—our anxieties, our ambitions, and the relentless pressure to prove our worth. We try to fill every second, terrified of the void.

An older gentleman taught me that emptiness is not always a loss. Sometimes, the empty space is exactly what saves us.

AI will continue to accelerate the world. But we must learn to be the empty cup. Let the blank space be your anchor as we look ahead to what is next. Allow yourself to stop, and let the quietness do its work.
